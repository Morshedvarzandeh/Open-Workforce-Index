Five jobs, sixteen people on the roster, one rule: give each job to the cheapest person who is actually qualified for it. Not the best person available — the cheapest one who clears the bar. Nobody sends the principal engineer to reformat an email, and nobody sends the copywriter to cut metal.

Two problems this has to survive

Someone new joins every week

A new model arriving is a new hire with no track record. You cannot trust the CV, and you cannot afford a full assessment centre for every applicant.

So evaluation is per-person, not per-roster. The existing sixteen are untouched — their measured evidence stays exactly as it was, and re-running it would buy nothing. Only the newcomer is assessed, against the same fixed corpus, so results are comparable by construction.

And they get a three-task trial before the eleven-task assessment. Most bad hires fail the trial, and the trial costs a quarter of the full run:

Onboarding cost is O(one person), and it is bounded by the newcomer's own rate. A weekly cadence stays in the low single dollars.

Keeping the accumulated knowledge cheap

An organisation's memory usually gets more expensive as it grows. Three deliberate choices stop that here.

  • Knowledge lives in the index, not the prompt. Everything learned is a row queried by (worker, skill). It is never replayed into a context window, so a year of history adds nothing to the cost of a single call. This is the one that matters most — history stuffed into context is what makes accumulated knowledge expensive.
  • Public evidence is capped. A benchmark claiming ten thousand samples contributes at most eight pseudo-observations. Old, weak, plentiful evidence can never outvote a handful of things you actually measured.
  • Evidence is scoped and never re-derived. Observations are append-only and immutable. Nothing is recomputed when someone joins, and evidence measured on one skill is not consulted for another — so the working set stays small no matter how much history accrues.

What is real here

Prices are measured · ability is assumed

Every rate, context window, and cost figure on this board is real, imported from a published price file with its source URL and a content digest. Every routing decision is real, computed by the engine.

The ability estimates are not. No benchmark measures these exact configurations on these skills, so the pass rates are assumed and tagged vendor_reported, which the calibration discounts to a tenth of a reproduced observation.

That discount is why the quality floors here sit near 0.30 rather than 0.90. The confidence you may demand is bounded by the evidence you hold, not by how confident you would like to feel — a vendor's claim of 93% only supports a 95% lower bound around 0.33. Real measured runs are what raise that ceiling; a press release never does.
